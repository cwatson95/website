sub=Newton&rsquo;s law of gravitation between two spheres you can drag: the pull is equal and opposite, proportional to each mass, and falls as the square of the distance between their centres
---
  ===================================================================
  gravity-force-lab.html — Newton's law of gravitation between two
  spheres on a track: drag them, change their masses, and read the
  equal and opposite pull
  ===================================================================

  Remake of PhET Interactive Simulations' Gravity Force Lab (University
  of Colorado Boulder, phet.colorado.edu, CC-BY 4.0). New code; no PhET
  code or assets.  PhET's build 2.2.25 (docs/phets/gravity-force-lab_en.html)
  was read only for its model constants, each cited below as "PhET's value".
  Its sibling coulombs-law.html runs on the same inverse-square engine.

  Four views.  THE LAB is PhET's screen: two uniform spheres on a 10-m
  track, masses 10-1000 kg, dragged along a 0.1-m grid; a force arrow on
  each (equal and opposite, drawn to one printed scale), the force in
  PhET's decimal or scientific notation, a draggable metre ruler, and the
  switch between mass-scaled spheres (150 kg/m^3) and constant-size ones.
  THE INVERSE SQUARE: the same force against the distance on log-log axes
  (a straight line of slope -2), on linear axes, and as F r^2, which is
  flat at G m1 m2 — with the distances the spheres cannot reach shaded.
  THE MASS PRODUCT: the force over every pair of masses at the current
  distance (hyperbolas m1 m2 = F r^2 / G), the force against one mass
  (a straight line through the origin), and the strongest pull two equal
  masses can ever reach — m^2 for constant-size spheres, about m^(4/3)
  for real ones, because a heavier sphere is also a bigger one.
  LET THEM GO: what the lab's forces would do if the track let the
  spheres move — the radial two-body free fall from rest, in closed form:
  the centre of mass stays put, the lighter sphere covers more ground,
  and two lab-sized masses take about twelve hours to touch.

  WHAT IS EXACT
  -------------
  F = G m1 m2 / r^2 with G = 6.6743e-11 m^3 kg^-1 s^-2 (CODATA 2022, read
  from scipy.constants 1.18 in build/gravity-force-lab/derive.py).  r is the
  distance between centres, which for uniform spheres is exact (Newton's
  shell theorem), not an approximation.  The mass-scaled radius is
  (3m / 4 pi rho)^(1/3).  The free fall is the closed form
  t(r) = sqrt(r0^3 / 2GM) [sqrt(x(1-x)) + asin sqrt(1-x)], x = r/r0,
  inverted by bisection; derive.py checks it against scipy solve_ivp
  integrating both bodies (8e-12), and selfTest against an RK4 of its own.
  Every number is pinned in selfTest to build/gravity-force-lab/refvals.txt.

  WHAT IS MODELLED (PhET's choices, reproduced)
  ---------------------------------------------
  PhET's values: the density 150 kg/m^3, the constant-size radius 0.5 m,
  masses 10-1000 kg in 10-kg steps, centres on a 0.1-m grid within +-5 m,
  and the no-overlap rule — centres at least snap(r1 + r2 + 0.1 m) apart.
  Rounding r1 + r2 + 0.1 to the grid can cut the gap below 0.1 m; derive.py
  enumerates all 10,000 reachable mass pairs and the gap never falls below
  0.05004 m (140 and 940 kg), so the spheres never touch.  When a sphere
  grows into its neighbour, the neighbour is pushed aside, or, if it is
  against the end of the track, the grown sphere moves (PhET's rule;
  switching the size mode counts as m2 growing last, PhET's update order).
  PhET uses G = 6.67408e-11 (CODATA 2014), so its forces read 3.3e-5 lower.

  NOT MODELLED
  ------------
  PhET's cartoon pullers, sounds and screen-reader descriptions.  The
  Earth: the spheres sit in free space on a frictionless track.  In "let
  them go", nothing after the spheres touch.
